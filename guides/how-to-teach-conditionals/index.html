How to Teach Conditionals: Zero, First, and Second for A2-B1

A practical guide to teaching zero, first, and second conditionals in sequence — the will-in-the-if-clause trap, tense matching, and free printable worksheets for A2-B1 classes.

Conditionals are one of the few grammar points where teaching all three types in one lesson almost guarantees failure — the structures are close enough in appearance that students blend them into one blurry "if" pattern. The fix is strict separation: one conditional fully secure before the next one is introduced.

1. Start with zero conditional as a fact, not a hypothesis

Zero conditional (if + present simple, present simple) states general truths and scientific facts — "If you heat water to 100°C, it boils." Anchor it with facts students already agree with, not imagined situations, so the present-present pattern feels logical rather than arbitrary. This also pre-empts the most common error one level up: adding "will" here, where a fact needs no future marker at all.

2. Move to first conditional only once zero is automatic

First conditional (if + present simple, will + base verb) talks about real, likely future situations — "If it rains, I'll take an umbrella." The single hardest habit to install here is keeping the if-clause in present simple even though the meaning is future. Drill this explicitly with a fill-in-the-blank pattern before free production: "If it ___ (rain), I ___ (take) an umbrella" — students must resist the urge to write "will rain."

3. Introduce second conditional as a clean break from first, with a physical anchor

Second conditional (if + past simple, would + base verb) is for hypothetical or unlikely situations — "If I won the lottery, I would travel the world." Because the "past simple" here doesn't mean past time, make that explicit on day one: point to the timeline and say "this is not the past — it's imaginary now." Contrast a real first-conditional sentence with its hypothetical second-conditional twin side by side ("If it rains, I'll stay home" vs. "If I lived on the moon, I would stay home") so students feel the shift in likelihood, not just the tense change.

Common mistakes and how to catch them

  • Will in the if-clause: "If it will rain, I will take an umbrella." — the single most frequent conditional error at any level. Drill the rule as fixed: never "will" after "if," regardless of conditional type.
  • Mixing first and second conditional tenses: "If I have more time, I would travel." — treat each conditional as a complete, non-mixable pair; correcting one side isn't enough, check both clauses match.
  • "Would" in both clauses: "If I would have money, I would buy a car." — very common with speakers of Romance languages, where the conditional mood appears in both clauses. State explicitly: English never uses "would" in the if-clause.

A 45-minute lesson shape that works (per conditional)

  1. 5 min — Warm-up: elicit 2-3 examples of the target pattern from real or general knowledge, writing one on the board.
  2. 10 min — Present the structure with the timeline/likelihood visual appropriate to that conditional.
  3. 10 min — Controlled practice: complete-the-sentence exercises, checking both clauses for tense agreement.
  4. 10 min — Contrast drill: 4-5 sentences where students must justify why this conditional (not another) fits the context.
  5. 10 min — Freer practice: students write or say 3 original sentences using the target conditional about their own lives.
