How to Teach Functional English — and Why It Has No Grammar Worksheet

Why functional English topics like complaints, opinions and invitations have reading, practice and writing worksheets but no grammar sheet, and how to teach them.

By the Workshido Editorial Team

Most topics on Workshido come as a full set: a Grammar worksheet, a Reading, a Writing, often a Practice sheet and a Full Quiz. But some sets — Making Complaints, Asking for Clarification, Ordering Food, Making Invitations — have only Reading, Practice and Writing. That isn't a missing piece. These are Functional English topics, and for them a grammar worksheet is the wrong tool.

1. Grammar topics and functional topics are built differently

A grammar topic is organised around a form: the past simple, the passive, comparatives. Everything in the set serves that one structure. A functional topic is organised around a purpose — something people need to do with English in daily life: invite a friend, complain about a faulty product, disagree without offending, ask someone to repeat what they said.

2. Why there's no Grammar worksheet

The phrases cut across grammar

Look at the language for making suggestions: Let's + base verb, Why don't we + base verb, How about + -ing, We could + base verb. That's four different structures for one job — and Why don't we…? is a negative question that isn't negative at all. A grammar worksheet would have to teach four unrelated rules, or teach one of them out of the context that gives it meaning. Neither helps students make a suggestion.

They're learned as chunks

Fluent speakers don't build "Would it be possible to…?" from modal-verb rules in real time; they retrieve it whole, like a single word. Students who learn these expressions as fixed chunks use them sooner and more confidently than students who try to assemble them.

The real difficulty is choosing the right phrase

"I disagree" and "I see your point, but…" are both grammatically perfect. Only one keeps the conversation friendly. What students need to practise is appropriacy: register, softening, knowing what to say first and how to respond. The CEFR treats this as pragmatic competence — a separate area from grammatical accuracy — and it needs its own kind of practice.

3. Where the grammar went

It isn't missing; it lives in two places. First, inside the phrases: students meet would, could and -ing forms as part of an expression, before they need the rule behind them. Second, in its own sets. The modals behind polite requests are covered in how to teach modal verbs, and the connectors that hold an opinion together in how to teach linking words. If a group struggles to form Could you…?, teach the modal set first and come back to the functional set afterwards.

4. How a functional set works

Every functional set follows the same three steps, from meeting the language to using it:

  • Reading — meet the language in a real exchange. A conversation, an email or a discussion where the phrases are doing their job. Students see who says what, to whom, and how the other person responds.
  • Practice — choose the right phrase. In a functional set this sheet does the job a grammar worksheet does elsewhere: matching phrases to functions, choosing the most polite option, completing mini-dialogues and rewriting blunt sentences politely.
  • Writing — use it for real. A guided dialogue, email or short text with a planner and a checklist, so students produce the phrases in a complete piece of communication.

Teach them in that order: Reading → Practice → Writing. In grammar sets the Practice sheet is optional extra repetition; in a functional set it is the controlled-practice step, so don't skip it.

A 45-minute lesson shape that works

  1. 5 min — Set up the situation before any language and board what students would say.
  2. 10 min — Reading: gist first, then highlight every phrase that does the job.
  3. 10 min — Sort the phrases by function and from most direct to most polite.
  4. 10 min — Practice worksheet: the right phrase for the situation and the person.
  5. 10 min — Role-play in pairs, then swap roles. The writing task follows next lesson.

Common problems and how to fix them

  • Correct but too direct: "I want a refund." "Repeat, please." Draw a politeness scale on the board and have students move each sentence one step up: I'd like a refund, please. Sorry, could you repeat that?
  • One phrase for everything: every opinion starts with "I think". Add "use three different phrases" to the writing checklist.
  • Knowing the phrase but not the reply: students can invite but freeze at "Sorry, I can't make it." Always practise both sides of the exchange and swap roles.
  • Translating from their first language: polite formulas rarely transfer word for word. Ask students how they would say it in their own language and compare what each version does.
  • Right words, wrong tone: a polite phrase in a flat or impatient voice still sounds rude. Model it aloud and drill the intonation chorally before the role-play.
