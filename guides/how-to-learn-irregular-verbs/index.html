How to Learn English Irregular Verbs by Pattern (Not the Alphabet)

There are 129 common irregular verbs in English, and almost every list presents them the same unhelpful way: alphabetically. Sorted by pattern instead, most of them stop looking random.

Ask a student to memorise "bring, brought, brought — buy, bought, bought — catch, caught, caught" as three unrelated entries in an alphabetical list, and it takes weeks to stick, if it ever does. Show them that bring / buy / catch / fight / seek / teach / think all take the exact same -ought/-aught shape, and it's one rule instead of seven.

Every irregular verb in English falls into one of four groups, based on how the base form, past simple and past participle relate to each other. Learn the groups, then the sub-patterns inside the hardest group, and the list stops feeling like 129 separate facts.

The four groups

GroupPatternVerbs
AAll three forms are the same24
BPast simple = past participle (only the base form differs)56
CBase form = past participle (only the past simple differs)3
DAll three forms are different46

Groups A and C are the easiest — together they're only 27 verbs, and A barely requires any memorising at all since nothing changes. Group B is the biggest at 56 verbs, but it breaks into small, clean families once you stop reading it as one long list. Group D, the 46 verbs where every form is different, is the one worth spending real time on — and it has patterns too.

Group A — nothing changes (24 verbs)

The base form, past simple and past participle are identical: cut, cut, cut. These need almost no active memorising — the trap isn't the spelling, it's remembering that a form looks unchanged but the sentence still needs it to carry past meaning.

betburstcastcostcutfithithurt letputquitreadsetshutspreadsplit bidbroadcastknitridshedslitupsetwed

The one to watch: read. It's the only verb in this group where the spelling doesn't move but the pronunciation does — read in the present is /riːd/, but read in the past and past participle is pronounced /rɛd/, like "red". Students read it correctly on the page and then say it wrong out loud, because nothing in the spelling tells them the sound has changed.

Group B — the past forms match (56 verbs)

Only the base form is different; past simple and past participle are identical: buy → bought → bought. This is the biggest group, but most of it splits into small families that share an ending — teach these as sets, not as 56 individual items.

The −ought / −aught family (7 verbs)

bring→brought buy→bought catch→caught fight→fought seek→sought teach→taught think→thought

The −ept family (4 verbs)

keep→kept sleep→slept sweep→swept weep→wept

That's 11 of the 56 accounted for as two short rules instead of eleven separate items. The rest of Group B (bend, feel, find, hold, leave, lose, make, meet, say, sell, send, sit, stand, tell, understand, win and more) is more mixed, but is still worth drilling as a single visual list rather than mixed in alphabetically with Groups A, C and D.

Watch for: lead → led → led is in this group too, and it's a common written mix-up with the metal lead (pronounced /lɛd/) — the verb's base form is pronounced /liːd/, like "need".

Group C — only 3 verbs (the shortcut)

Base form and past participle match; only the past simple changes. This is the smallest group in the whole system, so it's worth memorising outright rather than pattern-matching:

become→became come→came run→ran

Group D — the hard 46, and their sub-patterns

All three forms are different: speak → spoke → spoken. This is the group every list calls "the ones you just have to memorise" — but even here, roughly half fall into three recognisable vowel-shift families.

The i → a → u vowel shift (8 verbs)

begin→began→begun drink→drank→drunk ring→rang→rung shrink→shrank→shrunk sing→sang→sung sink→sank→sunk spring→sprang→sprung swim→swam→swum

Once a student notices the vowel simply moves i → a → u across all three forms, these 8 verbs stop being 24 separate spellings and become one shape applied 8 times.

The −oke/−oken family (7 verbs)

break→broke→broken choose→chose→chosen freeze→froze→frozen speak→spoke→spoken steal→stole→stolen wake→woke→woken weave→wove→woven

The −ew/−own family (6 verbs)

blow→blew→blown draw→drew→drawn fly→flew→flown grow→grew→grown know→knew→known throw→threw→thrown

That's 21 of the 46 "hardest" verbs organised into three families. The rest — be, bite, do, drive, eat, fall, forbid, forget, forgive, give, go, hide, lie, mistake, ride, rise, see, shake, show, swear, take, tear, wear, withdraw, write — really do need to be learned individually, which is exactly why it helps to have already cleared 21 of the 46 out of the way first.

In class, drill one family at a time rather than the whole list at once: put the −ought/−aught family on the board, elicit the pattern before you confirm it, then move to the i–a–u shift the following lesson. Five verbs that share a rule are far more likely to stick than 25 that don't.
