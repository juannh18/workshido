How to Sequence an English Topic: Grammar, Reading, Writing, Quiz

A practical order for teaching any English topic across several lessons — one skill at a time, with the gap between classes doing part of the work.

Most teachers don't run out of worksheets — they run out of worksheets that connect. You find a good grammar sheet on one site, a reading text on another, and a writing task on a third, and the three never quite line up: different level, different sub-focus, different vocabulary. The topic gets taught, but in one crowded lesson that doesn't stick.

A more reliable approach is to treat every topic as a short sequence: one skill per lesson, in a fixed order, with the natural gap between classes doing part of the work. Here is the order we recommend, and the reasoning behind each step.

The order — and why it's in this order

The sequence moves from recognising a structure to producing it, and from controlled practice to free practice. Input comes before output; accuracy work comes before fluency work.

1. Grammar — meet the rule

Start here. The grammar worksheet isolates the target form with a clear explanation and controlled exercises — gap-fills, transformations, matching. Cognitive load is low because students aren't juggling meaning, context and production at once; they're just noticing the pattern.

In class: put two example sentences on the board and elicit the rule from the students before you confirm it — they remember a rule they helped build. Then the worksheet, then a quick peer-check before you take answers, so students catch each other's slips and you hear where the class actually is. This is the "I do / we do / you do" shape the Teacher Edition lesson plans follow.

2. Reading — see it working

In the next lesson, the same structure appears inside a real text. Students read for general meaning first, answer comprehension questions, and only then go back to notice the target form in context. This is the step where the grammar stops feeling like an arbitrary rule and starts feeling like something people actually say and write.

In class: set one gist question before students read ("Where is the family going?"). Take that, then the detailed questions, then a short noticing task: "Underline every example of the structure in the text — why is it used there?"

3. Writing — produce it

Production is the hardest step, so it comes once input is solid. The writing worksheets are guided — prompts, sentence frames and success criteria, not a blank page — so students are producing the target structure, not fighting the task.

In class: model one sentence or a short paragraph together. Put the success criteria on the board — the two or three things a good answer must contain. Students write independently, then self-check against the criteria before handing anything in.

Practice — optional reinforcement

Many topics also have a Practice worksheet: extra mixed exercises on the same point, from controlled to freer. Use it for a group that needs more repetition, as homework, as a fast-finisher task, or as a warm-up recap at the start of the next class. It is genuinely optional — skipping it is not cutting a corner.

4. Full Quiz — check it stuck

Where a topic has a Full Quiz, save it for last — and ideally leave a lesson's gap before it. A short delay before testing is what turns practice into memory: retrieving something you have half-forgotten strengthens it far more than reviewing something still fresh. The Quiz covers grammar, reading and writing together in one graded assessment, and comes with a complete answer key, so you can review answers with the class rather than just recording a score.

Why one skill per lesson, not all at once

Splitting a topic across lessons feels slower, but the gap between classes is doing real work. Students forget a little between Tuesday and Thursday, and having to recall the rule on Thursday is what makes it stick — spaced practice beats massed practice in almost every study on the subject. Cramming grammar, reading and writing for one topic into a single 45-minute lesson is an efficient use of the clock and an inefficient use of memory.

One skill per lesson also keeps cognitive load manageable at A1–A2, where students are still decoding much of what they read and hear.

Adapt it to your class

The order is a sensible default, not a rule:

  • Run a short writing task first as a diagnostic if you want to see what students can already do with the structure.
  • Front-load the reading when the topic is vocabulary-heavy and students need the words before the rule.
  • Combine grammar and reading in one lesson for a strong group that moves quickly.
  • Use the Quiz as a pre-test at the start of the topic and again at the end to show progress.
  • In a mixed-level class, keep everyone on the same grammar–reading–writing spine and hand the practice sheet to students who finish early.
